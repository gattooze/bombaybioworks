The carbon cycle

How we close the loop.

Agricultural residue is biological material. It absorbed CO2 from the atmosphere while it was alive. When it decomposes or burns, it releases that CO2 back. This is the natural carbon cycle: in and out.

Thermal conversion intercepts this cycle at the point of residue and converts the organic carbon into a stable mineral-like form. That carbon cannot decompose. It cannot burn. It stays where you put it.

The net result: carbon that was recently in the atmosphere is now in the soil, in the ground, or in a product that locks it there. The cycle is broken. The carbon is removed.

Permanence

The H/C ratio is the number that matters.

The European Biochar Council's permanence protocol uses the molar ratio of hydrogen to organic carbon (H/Corg) as the key indicator of carbon stability. The lower the ratio, the more aromatic and stable the carbon structure.

An H/C ratio below 0.4 qualifies for EBC's permanence classification. Our typical batch measures 0.32, well within the permanence threshold. This number is not an estimate or a model assumption. It is a measured value from each production batch.

The EBC uses this ratio to calculate a permanence factor: what fraction of the carbon is expected to remain in the soil after 100 years, 1,000 years, and beyond. At H/C 0.32, that fraction is very high. The carbon is effectively out of the carbon cycle on any policy-relevant timescale.

Emission profile

No combustion. No stack. No emission.

Our process generates synthesis gas as a byproduct of thermal conversion. That gas is captured and used to fuel the conversion unit itself. There is no external fuel input. There is no stack emission from burning external fossil fuel.

The alternative: residue that decomposes in the field over weeks, releasing the same carbon as CO2 and methane. Or residue burned openly, releasing it all in hours. Both scenarios deliver the same carbon to the atmosphere with no useful output.

Our lifecycle assessment accounts for all process inputs, transportation, and land application. The net figure is negative: we remove more carbon than the process emits.

How we document removal

Every removal claim is a number, not a narrative.

EBC-certified test protocol

The European Biochar Certificate protocol defines how permanence and quality are measured. We test to this standard. The H/C molar ratio measurement is the primary permanence indicator and is included in every batch certificate.

Lifecycle assessment (LCA)

Our net removal figure comes from a lifecycle assessment covering feedstock collection, thermal conversion, transportation to application site, and soil application. All stages are accounted for. No carbon cost is excluded.

Carbon registries and credit issuance

CDR credits from our biochar can be verified and issued through established carbon registries using biochar-specific methodologies. The combination of EBC permanence data and LCA forms the evidentiary basis for credit issuance.

Co-benefit: soil health

The removal is permanent. The soil gets better in the process.

Carbon removal is the primary purpose of soil-applied biochar. But the pore structure that makes biochar such an effective carbon sink also makes it an excellent soil amendment.

The same micropore network that achieves 900+ m2/g of surface area in our activated carbon products is present, in modified form, in our biochar. In soil, those pores retain water, host beneficial microbes, and hold nutrients that would otherwise leach away.

The agronomic benefit is measurable: water retention typically improves by 30-50% in sandy or degraded soils. pH is buffered. Fertilizer efficiency improves. These benefits accrue to farmers directly, independent of any carbon credit value.
